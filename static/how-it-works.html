How Idea Search works

Idea search → Keyword search → API (Swagger) →

You describe an invention in plain language. The app looks for existing patents that describe the same or a similar idea and shows, feature by feature, how each one overlaps with yours.

2. Why an AI model is involved

A plain keyword search can't do this job well, for two reasons:

  • Your words aren't patent words. You might write "dissolves" and "seaweed"; a patent says "water-soluble" and "alginate". If you paste your description straight into a patent search, every word must match, and you usually get no results or the wrong ones. The AI rewrites the idea in the vocabulary patent drafters use and adds synonyms.
  • Search results are sorted by keyword match, not by similarity. A search returns dozens of patents that share some words with your idea. The AI reads each one and decides how many of your idea's features it actually describes.

The AI never invents patents. Every patent shown comes from Google Patents, and every "feature found" verdict comes with a quote from the patent text so you can check it.

3. The five steps in detail

1Analyze the idea

AI, 1 call0 SerpApi credits~5 seconds

The AI reads your description and returns:

  • A title and a one-sentence technical summary.
  • 3–7 key features, labelled F1, F2, … These are the elements a patent claim for your idea would need, for example "F1: the capsule is made from a seaweed-derived material". They are the yardstick for everything that follows.
  • 4 Google Patents searches, each pairing the product with one distinctive feature, written in patent vocabulary.
  • Suggested patent classes (CPC), shown for information only. In testing these guesses were often wrong, so they are not used in the searches.

Nothing is spent yet. You can edit, add or remove features and searches before going on.

The searches use Google Patents syntax:

AB=("coffee capsule" OR "coffee pod" OR "beverage capsule");AB=(seaweed OR alginate OR algae*)
SyntaxMeaning
;separates groups; every group must match
ORany one of the alternatives inside a group
AB=( ) TI=( ) CL=( )the group must match in the abstract, title or claims
"two words"exact phrase
algae*any word starting with "algae"

Why field limits like AB=? Without them a word can match anywhere in a 50-page patent. In testing, (seaweed OR algae);(capsule OR pod) matched 131,000 patents, and the top results were mostly unrelated (microcapsules, tissue engineering). Limiting every group to abstracts and adding a "coffee" group brought it down to 80, many of them actual coffee capsules.

2Search Google Patents

SerpApi1 credit per searchruns in parallel

The 4 searches run at the same time, and the top 20 results of each are kept (you can change this under "Filters and settings"). Your filters (country, date, granted only) apply to every search.

If a search returns fewer than 5 results, the app automatically retries it once in a looser form, using 1 extra credit. It drops the last group, or lets the second group match anywhere in the text. The "Search runs" section of the results shows when this happened.

Results from all searches are merged, and a patent found by several searches appears once. A typical idea yields 40–80 unique candidates.

3Screen the candidates

AI, 1 call per 25 candidates0 credits

The AI reads only the title and the short search snippet of each candidate and gives it a score from 0 to 10:

ScoreMeaning
9–10essentially the same invention
6–8same problem and several of the key features
3–5same field, few of the features
0–2unrelated

The 10 highest-scoring candidates go on to detailed comparison. Ties are broken by how many searches found the patent. The rest stay available under "Other candidates".

Why screen first? Fetching a patent's full details costs a credit, so this cheap first pass decides which patents are worth it.

4Fetch patent details

SerpApi1 credit per patent

For each of the 10 selected patents the app fetches the abstract and the first claim. The first claim is the broadest legal definition of what the patent protects. For patents in other languages it uses Google's English translation of the claims. Some patents (for example many granted European ones) have no abstract, and then the search snippet is used instead.

5Compare feature by feature

AI, 1 call per 3 patents0 credits~30 s per batch

The AI compares each patent's abstract and first claim with every one of your features and marks it:

  • disclosed the patent clearly describes this feature
  • partly it describes something similar, broader or narrower
  • not found the feature isn't mentioned

Each verdict comes with a short quote from the patent as evidence. The AI also gives an overall similarity score from 0 to 100 and one or two sentences naming the main overlap and the main difference. Results appear on the page after each batch of 3, so you can start reading before the run finishes.

+USPTO search, shown separately

USPTO Open Data PortalfreeUS applications filed since 2001

Alongside Google Patents, the app also searches the US Patent and Trademark Office's own database. Its results appear in a separate USPTO tab and are never mixed with the Google Patents results.

  • Searches. Step 1 also writes 3 USPTO searches. The USPTO database can only search titles and bibliographic data (no abstracts or claims), so these searches are title-only and broader, for example TI=(capsul* OR pod* OR cartridg*) AND TI=(compost* OR biodegrad* OR seaweed). Only utility applications are searched, and a search that finds fewer than 5 is retried once with its first group only.
  • Screening works as in step 3, but on the title and patent classes (CPC), since there is no snippet. The top 5 go on to the comparison (changeable under "Filters and settings"; 0 skips USPTO).
  • Comparison works as in step 5. The abstract and first claim come from the USPTO's own full text of the granted patent, or of the published application if it hasn't been granted. When neither is available, the card says "Title only" and the verdict is low-confidence.

Why keep it separate? The two sources search differently (full text vs. titles), cover different ground (worldwide vs. US since 2001) and use different text for the comparison. Seeing them side by side shows what each one found that the other missed.

4. How to read the results

Similarity score

ScoreMeaning
90–100the same invention
70–89most of your key features
40–69a related approach
below 40same field only

"Your features vs. the compared patents"

For each of your features, this shows the strongest match among all compared patents: Found, Partly found or Not found, and in which patents. Features that are "Not found" in every compared patent are where your idea may be new. A feature found in some patent doesn't by itself mean your idea isn't new: what matters is whether a single patent combines all of your features.

Feature matrix

One row per patent, one column per feature (✓ / ~ / –). Hover a cell to see the quoted evidence. A row with ✓ in every column is the strongest warning sign.

Other candidates

Patents that were found but not compared in detail, with their screening score. "Compare" on any of them fetches its details and adds it to the results (about 1 credit).

6. Tips for better results

  • Describe how it works, not just what it is. Say what the parts are, how they interact, and what makes it different from existing products. "A capsule made of a seaweed-based film that dissolves in hot water" works better than "eco-friendly coffee pod".
  • Check the features before you search. If the AI misread your idea or added something you didn't say, fix it. Every comparison is measured against this list.
  • Edit the searches when results look off. Use short words with * (rotat*) instead of long phrases, add synonyms an engineer or lawyer would use, and use 2 groups rather than 3. Check "Search runs" to see which searches returned little.
  • Use generic, older terms too. Patents from the 1990s and 2000s say "portable electronic device" rather than "smartphone" and "network" rather than "cloud". Adding such terms helps find older patents.
  • Use the date filter ("Before" plus a date) to look only at patents filed before a given day, e.g. before your own filing date.

7. What it can't do

This is a screening tool, not a legal opinion. Before filing a patent or making a business decision, have a patent attorney or professional searcher review the results.
  • It reads only the abstract and the first claim, not the full description or the drawings, where many details live.
  • Recent applications are invisible. Patent applications are published about 18 months after filing, so anything filed recently can't be found by any tool.
  • The USPTO search only sees titles of US applications filed since 2001, so a patent with an unusual title is easy to miss there; Google Patents covers it by full text.
  • Patents only. Scientific papers, products on the market and other non-patent prior art are not searched.
  • It can miss well-known patents. In tests with famous patents described in plain words (slide-to-unlock, a bladeless fan, one-click buying), the app found closely related patents every time but missed the original famous patent each time, because the AI-written searches use modern wording. Editing the searches helps.
  • The AI can make mistakes. Check the quoted evidence for any patent that matters, and open the patent itself via its link.
  • Patent class suggestions (CPC) are unreliable and only shown for reference.

8. Under the hood

For developers. The app is a small Python (FastAPI) server with plain HTML pages; there is no build step.

PartRole

app/llm.pyGroq client. Forces the AI to answer in a fixed JSON format, paces calls to stay under the per-minute token limit, retries when rate-limited
app/serpapi.pySerpApi client for Google Patents search (google_patents) and patent details (google_patents_details)
app/uspto.pyUSPTO Open Data Portal client: application search, and abstract + first claim from the grant or publication XML
app/cache.pySaves every SerpApi and Groq response in cache/; delete that folder to clear it

The API keys (SERPAPI_API_KEY, GROQ_API_KEY, USPTO_API_KEY) stay on the server in .env; the browser never sees them. Each step is stateless: the page sends the output of one step as the input to the next. Full endpoint details are in the Swagger UI.
